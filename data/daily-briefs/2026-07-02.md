## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The outlet of Lake Timiscaming — where the upper basin drains into the main stem — eased back noticeably on July 2, coming in at 517 cubic metres per second after briefly spiking to 606 on July 1. That spike now looks like a short pulse rather than a trend reversal: we're back sub-600 today for the first time since July 1 broke the previous six-day sub-600 streak (June 25–30). We are now 62 days past the freshet's primary peak in early May. The current Timiscaming outflow at 517 m³/s is consistent with the broader post-peak recession, though "gradual and lumpy" is the best characterisation — it hasn't been on a clean downward slope for weeks.

The mid-valley tells a more complicated story. Mattawa's midnight gauge (roughly halfway down the valley, well above the dam chain) slipped 5 cm from its revised July 1 value to 152.56 m today — but it was still 27 cm higher than a week ago (152.29 m on June 25). That elevation reflects the recent precipitation pulse still washing through the system. Pembroke rose 6 cm to 111.43 m, and Des Joachims rose 9 cm — both consistent with mid-valley flow rising even as the upper-basin source gauge at Timiscaming eases. The precipitation signal is in the plumbing, not yet fully spent.

Most significantly, the Ottawa River Regulation Planning Board published an unscheduled new forecast today (July 2, 12:41 PM EDT), ahead of the July 7 date that had been listed on the June 30 page. The update reversed the June 30 forecast: where June 30 called for "continued decreasing gradually or remain fairly stable," today's forecast explicitly says "water levels and flows are expected to increase gradually over the next few days" and cites "significant precipitation received across the basin recently." ORRPB pushing an early update before the scheduled date is itself a signal of operational awareness — they're watching this event closely. The basin's storage reservoirs shifted to a more settled posture today: three rising, ten steady, one falling — down from six rising yesterday. That partly reflects the July 1 pulse passing through, with the precipitation-driven response not yet fully loaded into the reservoir tier.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The government telemetry gauge at the Mansfield waterfront on Lac Coulonge — a distinct measurement point on the lake itself, not at the dam — read 106.30 metres at noon Eastern time today. That is about 11 centimetres above the same hour yesterday (106.19 m), and the midnight-to-midnight ORRPB series confirms the same direction: from 106.21 m (July 1 midnight) to 106.29 m (July 2 midnight), a rise of 8 centimetres. The lake has now risen about 19 cm over the past two days after sitting in a tight band around 106.11–106.13 m for most of the preceding week. That said, the lake remains firmly in normal condition: it is 70 centimetres below the pre-alert advisory level of 107.00 metres, and about 120 centimetres below the minor-flood threshold. No flood concern exists.

Further down from the lake, at the Bryson Generating Station where the south channel of Lac Coulonge eventually narrows and winds to the dam site well below the Mansfield properties, turbines continued into their fourth day of operation on July 2. Total throughput was 572 m³/s — split roughly evenly between turbines (283 m³/s) and the spillway (288 m³/s). The dam pond level edged down just 2 cm to 105.27 metres, still 60 centimetres above the permitted upper ceiling of 104.67 m (the 35th consecutive day above that ceiling). The drawdown rate has slowed from the first two turbine days because upstream inflow from the mid-valley pulse is largely offsetting what the turbines drain. With ORRPB now explicitly forecasting increases across the main stem over the next few days, property owners should expect the current gradual rise at Lac Coulonge to continue and possibly steepen, though the 70-cm buffer to the pre-alert threshold provides considerable margin before any advisory would be warranted.
