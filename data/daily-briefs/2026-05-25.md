The level has climbed another 25 centimetres since yesterday midnight, reaching 150.93 m as of this afternoon. The operator is absorbing inflow from the northern basin rather than releasing it straight downstream. That is standard post-freshet refill behaviour, not a problem.

Of the 14 tracked headwater reservoirs, 3 are rising, 9 are holding steady, and 2 are falling modestly (the most recent data covers May 23 to May 24). Des Joachims is the only one exceeding 10 centimetres per day. Rising reservoir levels during recession mean operators are catching and storing inflow rather than passing it all downstream — this is exactly what is supposed to happen and it slightly reduces the water volume arriving at the property.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The lake at Fort-Coulonge and Mansfield is on the doorstep of its last milestone. The gauge reads approximately 107.03 metres — only 3 centimetres above the 107.00-metre threshold that marks the transition from an elevated-watch (surveillance) notice down to the lowest active flag level (pre-alert). The lake dropped 19 centimetres since midnight, and at the current pace it could clear this final threshold overnight or by early tomorrow morning. Once it does, the property will be below every named flood alert level for the first time since the spring freshet peak, though "below the threshold" still means the lake is elevated compared to a typical spring.

The dam at the foot of Lac Coulonge (Bryson Generating Station) cut its total release another 6.4% today, once again almost entirely through the spillway (down 8.9%). This is the water level driving the reduction: as the lake drops, less water presses over the spillway. The dam's backed-up pond between the lake and the generating station has now fallen to 103.94 metres — 26 centimetres below its normal operating floor of 104.20 metres, and deeper than yesterday's 20-centimetre breach. The turbines remain at essentially the same output. There is nothing unusual in the operators' actions; the headpond is passively tracking the lake level down.

The river management board last updated its forecast on May 21 and switched to weekly bulletins; the next update is tomorrow, Tuesday May 26. That May 21 text said to expect rapid decline with no major precipitation, which is exactly what is happening. The bottom line for the property: the water is going down at roughly 19 centimetres per day, the property's last active flood notice is 3 centimetres away, and nothing in the basin's current posture suggests any reversal in the short term.

One note from the lower basin that has no direct daily impact on the property gauge but remains the project's main regulatory point of interest: the regulatory ceiling at the basin's terminal dam — Carillon, near Grenville, QC, which controls water flow toward the Montreal region — has now been exceeded for 21 consecutive days. The trigger gauge at the Hull dock (which activates the spring-flood ceiling requirement) reads 42.74 metres, still 13 centimetres above the 42.61-metre level that would deactivate it. At its current rate of decline, Hull could drop below that threshold in roughly 1–2 days, which would end the formal ceiling period.
