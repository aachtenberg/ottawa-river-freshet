## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The upper Ottawa basin is on Day 47 of its post-peak recession, and the story this week has been an unexpectedly persistent third pulse at Mattawa — the town just below Lake Timiscaming where the northern half of the basin drains into the main stem. That pulse remains active: Mattawa read 152.66 metres today, 29 centimetres above where it stood a week ago. The third rise that first became visible last week has neither crested nor retreated; it is simply sitting near a local plateau roughly comparable to the secondary June 12 pulse peak (152.61 m), with day-to-day variation but no clear direction either up or down.

The source of that persistence is Témiscaming outflow. Using the daily midnight values compiled by the regulator, outflow has been below 700 cubic metres per second for nine consecutive days — but today the ORRPB's own conditions page showed an intraday reading of 751 cubic metres per second at 9 AM, suggesting the outflow has been fluctuating near the 700 mark and may be climbing. The big-picture direction is mildly upward: the daily midnight figure went from 666 yesterday to 684–688 today. There is also an important data correction from yesterday's brief: the preliminary June 16 reading of 704 m³/s that appeared to end the sub-700 streak has since been revised down to 666 m³/s, confirming that the streak in fact continued unbroken through yesterday. Today's daily value (684–688 m³/s) keeps the streak alive for a ninth day.

Below Mattawa, the Des Joachims impoundment — a large Ontario Power Generation reservoir between Mattawa and Pembroke — is processing an elevated pulse, with its outflow rising from about 802 to 996 cubic metres per second overnight while its reservoir level continued to creep upward. That water is moving downstream and is expected to reach Pembroke in the next day or two. Farther south, the Gatineau River — a major western tributary — is easing quickly for a second consecutive day: the Paugan station above the river mouth fell nearly 16 per cent, and the Rapides-Farmers station at the Gatineau's confluence with the Ottawa fell more than 10 per cent. These reductions will help offset the mid-valley pulse arriving from Des Joachims at the basin's lower stations.

The ORRPB finally updated its conditions forecast today, for the first time since June 9. The new forecast acknowledges forecast precipitation and now says levels and flows may "increase gradually or remain fairly stable" — replacing last week's "decrease gradually" language and adding a caution that "rapid increases are possible if heavy rainfall occurs." The next update is now scheduled for June 23, confirming a weekly posting cadence rather than daily.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The lake at the foot of Lac Coulonge is rising — a notable reversal from the gentle decline that had been underway. At 4 PM local time today, the Fort-Coulonge gauge read 106.27 metres, roughly 11 centimetres above the same time yesterday. Overnight, the midnight-to-midnight change was plus 5 centimetres. The lake remains well within normal seasonal range — the lowest alert level (107.00 metres, sometimes called "pre-alert") is still 73 centimetres above the current surface — but the direction has changed from down to up.

The most likely driver is the dam at the foot of the lake. Bryson Generating Station entered its third day of a near-total turbine shutdown: only about 35 cubic metres per second of the dam's roughly 506-tonne total flow is passing through the turbines, with 93 per cent going through the open spillway instead. Critically, the dam's pond — which sits immediately upstream — rose 10 centimetres today to 105.53 metres, not only reversing yesterday's modest 4-centimetre decline but setting a new high since the dam's operating limits were first exceeded on May 29. This rising headpond represents backwater pressure that pushes lake levels upstream toward Fort-Coulonge.

At 86 centimetres above its normal operating ceiling of 104.67 metres and on its twentieth consecutive day outside the permitted band, the Bryson headpond remains the most significant ongoing operational anomaly in the basin. Recovery requires the turbines to restart. No public information has emerged about a timeline for that restart. The ORRPB's new forecast note about possible gradual increases if precipitation occurs adds a further wrinkle: if rain raises inflows to the headpond, the current spillway-only configuration may not be able to hold the pond level stable.
