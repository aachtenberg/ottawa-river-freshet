## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The Ottawa basin's upper-valley recession has taken another step toward summer calm. The outflow from Lake Timiscaming — the large lake straddling the Ontario–Quebec border that drains into the Ottawa main stem — has now crossed below 500 cubic metres per second for the first time since the Canada Day weekend surge. At 492 m³/s today, the outflow is now only modestly above the long-term early-July baseline of roughly 400–450 m³/s, and both the official federal gauge and the ORRPB table agree on that number without any discrepancy worth flagging. This is Day 70 since the spring freshet peak in early May — the basin is deep into its normal summer transition.

The mid-valley stations are in steady decline. The town of Mattawa — roughly two-thirds of the way between Lake Timiscaming and the dam chain — bounced slightly upward on July 9 (a 12-centimetre uptick, probably residual wave tail from the Canada Day surge), but July 10 shows a 8-centimetre drop, resuming the downward trend. Pembroke, farther downstream, continued its clean fall with another 9-centimetre drop today, its third consecutive daily decline. The most striking signal today came from the Britannia gauge near Ottawa, which fell 15 centimetres in a single day — the clearest sign yet that the lower-valley bulge from the Canada Day event has fully passed through.

The basin's storage reservoirs are in a remarkably stable state. Only one of the fourteen tracked reservoirs fell by more than 2 centimetres overnight (Poisson Blanc, −3 cm); the other thirteen are essentially flat. This near-perfect balance — inflows and outflows nearly equalized across the basin — is exactly what the post-freshet summer equilibrium looks like. No reservoir is actively rising in a way that would suggest operators are building storage or resisting recession.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

Lac Coulonge has paused its rapid recession and is essentially flat today. The Vigilance gauge at the Mansfield waterfront read 106.238 metres at 4 PM Eastern on July 10 — about 1.4 centimetres *above* the same reading 24 hours earlier. The ORRPB midnight series confirms this pause: the lake fell only 1 centimetre between the July 9 and July 10 midnight readings, a dramatic shift from the 15-centimetre single-day plunge on July 8. This is not a flood concern — the lake is still more than three-quarters of a metre below the 107.00-metre level at which a pre-alert advisory would begin to apply, and well over a metre below the 107.50-metre minor-flood threshold. But the rate change is notable: yesterday's brief recorded the fastest 24-hour recession of the event; today the lake has essentially levelled off.

The dam pond at the Bryson Generating Station — located well below the property reach, reached by following Lac Coulonge's south-end narrows several kilometres down to the dam — also ticked up 4 centimetres today (105.36 metres at 3 PM Eastern). The dam pond has now been above its operating ceiling of 104.67 metres for 43 consecutive days. Hydro-Québec's total release at Bryson is essentially unchanged from yesterday at about 497 cubic metres per second, split roughly 58% through the turbines and 42% through the spillway. However, the dam just upstream — Rapide-7 — has been oscillating sharply, a pattern that wasn't fully visible in yesterday's brief due to a routine one-day lag in the data feed: between July 8 and July 9, Rapide-7 jumped from 156 to 235 cubic metres per second (+51%) and started spilling for the first time since the July 8 cuts. Today it edged slightly lower (230 m³/s) but shifted more flow from the turbines to the spillway (now at 66% spill share vs 54% yesterday). This kind of turbine-to-spill shift within a nearly constant total can reflect scheduled maintenance, load dispatch changes, or reservoir level management — it does not change the overall amount of water passing through the dam chain.

The ORRPB forecast remains unchanged since July 7 and the board has formally switched to a weekly update schedule, with the next forecast expected July 14. Today's data is consistent with the July 7 text: conditions are near seasonal values and gradually declining. At the current near-flat rate of change at Lac Coulonge, the lake could stabilise here or continue a slow decline — with no precipitation in the forecast, there is no flood concern for the foreseeable future.
