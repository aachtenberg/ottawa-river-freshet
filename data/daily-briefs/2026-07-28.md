## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The most consequential upstream development today is also the simplest to summarise: it rained. Outflow from Lake Timiscaming — the large natural lake at the head of the Ottawa River system — jumped from about 322 cubic metres per second yesterday to 381 m³/s today, the largest single-day increase since the Canada Day surge in early July. A cross-check measurement from a separate instrument on the same lake gives 351 m³/s — a 30 m³/s gap that falls well within the normal uncertainty on first-published daily figures, and both instruments agree the direction is sharply up. The Ottawa River Regulation Planning Board confirmed the cause in its first forecast update in a week, published this afternoon: "recent rainfall and additional precipitation expected across the basin today and tomorrow." This is the first change to the ORRPB's forecast text since July 21, and it represents a meaningful shift in tone — from "expected to remain fairly stable" last week to "expected to increase gradually or remain fairly stable depending on locations" this week.

Despite the rainfall pulse, the fourteen tracked storage reservoirs across the watershed are almost universally holding still. Thirteen of them moved less than two centimetres in either direction. The one exception is Lake Timiscaming itself, which rose five centimetres today even as its outflow jumped — a sign that rainfall is adding water to the lake faster than it is being released downstream, the natural buffering effect you'd expect at the start of a rain event. There is no sign of coordinated storage release or active retention across the basin; operators are letting the system absorb rainfall at a normal pace.

Mid-valley monitoring stations show a mixed and still-provisional picture. The gauge at the town of Mattawa confirms today what was expected: yesterday's dramatic provisional drop (to 152.19 m) was revised upward sixteen centimetres this morning to 152.35 m — precisely the same revision pattern as the day before. The current first-published daily value for today, 152.39 m, is up four centimetres from the revised yesterday figure. Given the established pattern, this may itself be revised tomorrow. The Pembroke gauge, further downstream, shows an essentially flat reading of 111.06 m, down a single centimetre from yesterday — a reliable and stable number.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The lake gauge at Fort-Coulonge reads 106.00 metres as of 4 PM local time today — down about four centimetres from the same time yesterday. This is fully normal summer territory, sitting approximately one hundred centimetres below the lowest flood-advisory level of 107.00 metres, and there is no flood concern of any kind. The ORRPB midnight series, which uses a midnight snapshot rather than an afternoon reading, also shows 106.00 m for today — five centimetres below the revised midnight value from July 27. The lake's gentle post-event easing continues.

At the Bryson Generating Station — which sits several kilometres downstream of where Lac Coulonge's southern arm narrows and winds its way down to the dam — operators dialled back the spillway a step today, from about 159 cubic metres per second yesterday to about 121 m³/s today. Despite this continued high spill, the dam pond behind the station unexpectedly rose five centimetres rather than falling. This is the rainfall making itself felt: the Coulonge River watershed and other tributaries that drain into the dam pond are delivering more water than the 121 m³/s spillway can drain away. The dam pond now sits 61 consecutive days above its normal summer operating ceiling of 104.67 m, and at 105.36 m it is 69 centimetres above that ceiling — slightly more than yesterday's 64 centimetres. The drawdown that began two days ago has paused, at least for today.

The ORRPB's updated forecast says that with rain today and tomorrow, water levels at some locations along the main stem are "expected to increase gradually." For the lake at Fort-Coulonge, the story is more nuanced than that: Bryson's operators control the key lever. If they maintain or increase spill tomorrow, the lake's gentle decline can continue even through a regional rain event. If they reduce spill to protect the dam pond from further rise, the lake could stabilise or tick slightly upward. The basin is not facing any flood risk — the lake is a metre from the lowest advisory threshold — but the dam pond's prolonged above-ceiling state means operators will be managing carefully through the precipitation.
