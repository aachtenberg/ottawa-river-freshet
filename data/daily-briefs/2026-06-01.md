The recession at Lake Témiscaming has entered a plateau. After thirty days of fairly steady decline from the May 2 peak near 2,741 cubic metres per second, the outflow from the lake has barely moved over the past four days — holding between roughly 1,200 and 1,240 m³/s. The proxy database recorded an unusually low reading of 362 m³/s this morning, which is almost certainly a partial-day average captured in the early hours before the daily value had accumulated; the ORRPB conditions website showed 1,234 m³/s at 2 PM Eastern, far more consistent with the slow-recession trend. Tomorrow's finalized daily average will clarify, but at the current plateau rate the sub-1,200 threshold is no longer imminent — it could take a week or more, or the plateau could hold at this level for some time.

Mid-valley, the Des Joachims pulse that OPG sent downstream on May 30 continues to track through the system. Mattawa (roughly halfway between Lake Témiscaming and the property) rose another 12 centimetres overnight for a fourth consecutive day of increases — up nearly 30 centimetres from its low point on May 28. Pembroke (about two-thirds of the way downstream) held flat after rising 3 centimetres the previous day: the pulse is passing through. At 14 reservoirs monitored across the basin, 13 are essentially flat (within ±2 cm) and one — the Des Joachims reservoir itself — rose another 10 centimetres, reflecting OPG's continued storage management as it gradually works down the elevated level accumulated since late May.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The river at Fort-Coulonge slowed its descent to near zero overnight — dropping just 2 centimetres compared to 9 centimetres the previous day. Afternoon sensor readings confirmed the water level at approximately 106.35 metres, still 64 centimetres below the first warning threshold (the "pre-alert" level of 107.00 m) and 114 centimetres below minor flood (107.50 m). All warning flags remain off and conditions are fully normal. The near-stall in the decline rate is the clearest sign yet that the downstream pulse from Des Joachims has reached the upper reach of Lac Coulonge: the extra water arriving from upstream is offsetting the natural tail-off of the recession. This plateau in the decline rate is expected to last another day or two as the pulse passes through.

The dam pond at the foot of Lac Coulonge — Bryson Generating Station's headpond — rose another 12 centimetres and is now at 105.20 metres, 53 centimetres above the top of its normal operating range (104.20 to 104.67 metres). This is Day 4 of the upper-band breach, and the rate of rise has actually accelerated slightly over the past three days. The operator's posture is essentially locked: about three-quarters of all water is bypassing the turbines and flowing through the spillway, while turbine output has barely moved. Despite routing most of the water through the spillway, the headpond continues rising — inflow still exceeds what the spillway can pass at the current opening. The Des Joachims pulse, which is now passing Pembroke about two days upstream of Lac Coulonge, will likely sustain elevated inflow into at least June 2–3 before any easing.

At the basin's downstream terminal — the Carillon dam near Hawkesbury — a notable operational shift occurred today. Total releases fell about 6%, but beneath that headline number a more significant rebalancing took place: turbine output was cut nearly 20% while spillway flow nearly doubled (from 508 to 800 cubic metres per second, up 57%). The regulatory trigger gauge at the Hull dock in Gatineau continued its gradual descent to 42.03 metres, now 58 centimetres below the threshold (42.61 m) that would impose the strict headpond ceiling at Carillon. That ceiling is not in effect for a seventh consecutive day.

## Notes

- **Bryson headpond: what would break the stalemate?** The current pattern (total ~540 m³/s, 75% spill, headpond rising ~10 cm/day) has been running four days. For the headpond to stop rising, total outflow would need to increase by roughly 30–50 m³/s (beyond current inflow), which would require either opening the spillway further or restarting turbine generation. Once the Des Joachims pulse passes (expected June 3–4), inflow pressure should ease and the headpond may begin to fall naturally. Conversely, if the headpond reaches ~105.5–105.7 m — roughly 1 metre above normal maximum — the operator would likely need to increase total release.

- **Témiscaming plateau implications for case file:** The ~1,200–1,240 m³/s plateau over four days (May 29–June 1) raises the question of whether this is (a) a natural inflection in the recession curve as the basin drains to seasonal base flow, or (b) a managed target reflecting PSPC's deliberate refill of Lake Témiscaming ahead of summer.
