## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The dominant story in the basin today is not the upper watershed — it is the mid-valley, where daily average flows at two main-stem stations have nearly tripled compared to yesterday. The gauging point at the Des Joachims Generating Station recorded a running daily average of 995 cubic metres per second through 3 PM Eastern today, up from 377 cubic metres per second yesterday — an increase of 164%. The nearby Otto Holden station, just upstream, went from 347 to 740 cubic metres per second (+113%). The source of this surge is not the upper watershed: Lake Timiscaming, at the top of the main stem, is releasing a steady 327–330 cubic metres per second (its 24th straight day below 400), so the extra 600+ cubic metres per second must be arriving from mid-valley tributaries — likely the Petawawa River and other highland streams in west-central Ontario that collect large rainfall catchments and whose peak flows from the August 2–3 rain event are now reaching the main stem.

This surge has not yet appeared at Pembroke (the next major gauge downstream, currently 111.06 metres and slowly falling), nor at Fort-Coulonge (the property gauge, at 106.08 metres and flat). The wave is in transit. Based on normal travel times, it could begin lifting Pembroke within 12–24 hours and could start affecting the Fort-Coulonge reach — and with it the Lac Coulonge property stretch — within roughly 1 to 3 days, depending on whether the inflow sustains or peaks today.

Mattawa — where the Mattawa River joins the Ottawa main stem at the upstream end of the Des Joachims pool — read 152.44 metres at 2 PM Eastern today (live reading), up from a midnight level of about 152.37 metres, a modest 7-centimetre rise. This is consistent with slightly elevated inflow arriving through the pool. The ORRPB midnight table for August 5 also shows 152.44 metres in its current-day column; as noted in prior briefs, this column for the current (not-yet-complete) day tends to reflect the live reading rather than a true midnight value, and the database midnight value is 152.30 metres. In any case, the direction is upward today at Mattawa, and the mid-valley surge is the reason.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

For now, the property stretch is calm. The gauge at Fort-Coulonge — the Vigilance station that monitors the lake level at the property reach — read 106.08 metres at 4 PM local time, essentially unchanged from yesterday's 106.10 metres and essentially unchanged from the last six days (the lake has been ranging 106.08–106.10 metres since July 31). The lake is 92 centimetres below the pre-alert threshold and nearly 1.4 metres below the minor-flood threshold. There is no active flood concern.

However, the mid-valley surge described above is the single most important forward-looking signal for the property today. If the extra 600+ cubic metres per second arriving at Des Joachims propagates downstream without being absorbed by operators at Chenaux (the next downstream generating station after Pembroke), it will arrive at the Fort-Coulonge reach. Chenaux has already increased its own daily average release today (623 to 750 cubic metres per second, up 20%), which is partly absorbing the wave — but the full picture depends on total tributary inflow, which ORRPB has not yet had time to update its forecast around (the forecast was written yesterday and is not due to be updated until August 11). Watch Pembroke over the next 24 hours; if it begins to rise, Fort-Coulonge is likely to follow 1–2 days later.

The dam pond at the Bryson Generating Station — located well downstream of where Lac Coulonge's south end narrows and winds toward the dam, not visible from or adjacent to the Mansfield and Fort-Coulonge properties — saw an abrupt and dramatic operating change late in the afternoon today. Between 6 PM and 7 PM Eastern time, operators cut turbine output from about 267 to 137 cubic metres per second (nearly in half) while simultaneously surging the spillway from 174 to 348 cubic metres per second. Total discharge increased slightly (from 441 to 485 cubic metres per second), but the composition reversed: yesterday the dam was 68% turbine / 32% spillway; by 7 PM today it was 28% turbine / 72% spillway. The headpond at the dam pond stood at 105.39 metres at 7 PM — essentially unchanged from yesterday (105.38 metres), having moved by only 1 centimetre. This sharp posture change bears watching through tomorrow: if the elevated spillway rate is sustained, it will begin to draw down the headpond (the dam pond is still 72 centimetres above its normal summer operating ceiling and has been continuously above that ceiling for 69 days).

The downstream picture at Carillon — the basin's terminal dam roughly 300 kilometres to the southeast — is a mild improvement from yesterday. Total flow there settled to about 2,011 cubic metres per second at 7 PM Eastern, and the spillway that opened for the first time in this episode yesterday (releasing 101 cubic metres per second over the gates) has been closed today. The turbines are handling all of the flow again. The level at the Hull waterfront gauge — the trigger point for the spring-flood directive that sets strict operating limits at Carillon — held at 41.51–41.52 metres, 110 centimetres below the 42.61-metre threshold, continuing a plateau that began August 3.

- **Mid-valley surge context and property forecast.** The Des Joachims/Otto Holden surge (tripling of daily flow) is the story to watch for the next 72 hours. The ORRPB forecast says "decreasing gradually over the coming week" but was written before this development. If this is purely delayed runoff from the Aug 2–3 rainfall, the surge may crest quickly and dissipate before significantly affecting Fort-Coulonge. If it reflects a second rain event or an extended catchment response, the property stretch could see a modest lake rise. The baseline is favorable: Fort-Coulonge is at 106.08 m, nearly a full metre below the pre-alert threshold. A surge of even 30–50 cm would still leave the property well in normal range. But 50+ cm is possible if the mid-valley wave propagates strongly through Chenaux to Carillon and forces Bryson to increase releases.

- **Bryson abrupt posture change.** The sharp turbine cut at T19 (half-capacity in one hour) is unusual and cannot be explained by the data alone. Possible explanations: (a) grid demand dropped sharply and dispatch reduced turbine output; (b) a turbine unit went offline for scheduled or unscheduled maintenance; (c) operators proactively increased spill to manage headpond ahead of anticipated high inflow from the mid-valley surge. Reason (c) would be notable because it would imply operators are already responding to the mid-valley signal — something the ORRPB hasn't yet commented on. Watch tomorrow's T19 Bryson reading: if turbines remain at ~137 m³/s, the reason is likely structural (maintenance). If they rebound to 250–300 m³/s with spill falling, it was temporary grid dispatch.
