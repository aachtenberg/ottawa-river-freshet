## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The flow leaving Lake Temiscaming — the upper Ottawa's snowmelt pulse working its way south — edged up slightly today to 1,492 cubic metres per second (m³/s), from 1,465 m³/s yesterday. The uptick is small (27 m³/s, less than 2%), but it breaks an 11-day consecutive decline streak that had run from May 13 through May 23. Today's figure is preliminary — ORRPB's official numbers often get revised overnight — so this might well be revised back down by tomorrow's brief. Even so, the broader picture is unchanged: the flow leaving Lake Temiscaming is 46% below the freshet peak of roughly 2,741 m³/s set on May 2, and we are now 22 days past that high-water mark. The basin is deep into post-freshet recession.

The mid-valley picture is mixed today in a way that likely has the same explanation as the Temiscaming uptick. At Mattawa — the level gauge that tracks the Ottawa as it descends from the northern basin — the river showed a slight afternoon uptick of 10 centimetres, pausing after four consecutive days of decline from the May 19 rain-event high. Pembroke, further downstream, is still falling (down another 12 centimetres — its fourth straight day of decline). At the Des Joachims reservoir and generating station, situated between Mattawa and Pembroke, OPG accumulated another 15 centimetres of water in the reservoir today — the biggest single-day storage jump this week. The operator released 1,807 m³/s from Des Joachims, yet the level still rose, meaning inflow from the northern basin exceeded that outflow considerably. OPG is absorbing this pulse into storage rather than routing it all downstream.

Of the 14 tracked headwater reservoirs, 5 are rising, 7 are flat, and 2 are falling modestly. Only Des Joachims exceeded 10 centimetres today; the others are in the small steps typical of post-freshet refill (Dozois +3 cm, Mitchinamecus +4 cm, Lake Temiscaming itself +2 cm). Rising reservoir levels during recession mean operators are catching and storing inflow rather than releasing everything downstream — this is normal, expected, and slightly reduces the downstream flow pulse.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The lake at Fort-Coulonge and Mansfield is in an accelerating decline. The property's gauge reads approximately 107.15 metres as of midnight tonight — 35 centimetres below the minor-flood threshold it cleared yesterday, and now only 15 centimetres above the pre-alert threshold of 107.00 metres. The lake is dropping at roughly 22 centimetres per day, faster than the 15 cm/day pace of the past week. If this rate holds, the gauge could drop below 107.00 metres — effectively exiting even the elevated-watch zone — sometime tomorrow afternoon. That would be the final flood-state milestone: below 107.00 metres is the lowest active flag level, meaning the lake is back to elevated-but-not-flooded conditions.

The dam at the foot of Lac Coulonge (Bryson Generating Station) trimmed its total release by another 6% overnight, almost entirely by reducing spillway flow. This is straightforward physics: as the lake level drops, there is less water pressure pushing flow over the spillway. More worth watching is the condition of the dam's backed-up pond (the headpond) between the lake and the dam structure — it has now dropped to 104.00 metres, sitting 20 centimetres below its normal operating floor of 104.20 metres. This breach has more than doubled in one day (9 cm below yesterday morning, 20 cm below tonight). The operators are not doing anything unusual; the headpond is passively following the lake down. But 20 cm below the band floor is a notable departure from normal, and the rate of deepening is accelerating.

The river management board's most recent forecast — from its May 21 final daily bulletin — called for rapid decline with no major precipitation in sight, and that is exactly what is happening. The board has now switched to a weekly update schedule (next bulletin Tuesday May 26); no new text has been issued since May 21. The bottom line for the property owner is straightforward: the water is going down at an accelerating pace, the pre-alert level exit is likely tomorrow, and nothing in the basin's posture or the forecast suggests any reversal.

One note from the lower basin: the regulatory situation at the basin's terminal dam (Carillon, near Grenville, QC) continues. The trigger gauge at the Hull dock reads 42.82 metres — 21 centimetres above the 42.61-metre level that activates the spring-flood operating ceiling. With that trigger active, Carillon's headpond is supposed to stay below 40.08 metres; it is currently at 40.51 metres, 43 centimetres above that ceiling. This is the 20th consecutive day of this overshoot and has no direct day-to-day impact on the property gauge, but it remains the project's primary regulatory data point.
