## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

Outflow from Lake Timiscaming — the large natural lake at the head of the Ottawa River system — is holding near 340 cubic metres per second, about twelve percent of the peak flow recorded during the May 2 spring flood and now fifteen straight days below the 400 m³/s mark. Two independent measurements give slightly different readings (341 and 332 m³/s), which is within normal day-one data uncertainty; both agree this is late-summer baseflow territory. The week-over-week trend is essentially flat — the same as last Monday.

The fourteen tracked storage reservoirs scattered across the basin remain almost completely still: thirteen of them moved less than two centimetres in either direction from yesterday. The one exception, the reservoir just above the Des Joachims generating station on the main stem, rose five centimetres — a minor and routine fluctuation in normal operations. There is no bulk storage release or absorption happening anywhere in the system right now.

The water-level gauge at the town of Mattawa, about halfway down the river, again shows a large single-day drop today — nineteen centimetres — to 152.19 metres. Based on yesterday's experience, this almost certainly reflects a provisional first-report value that will be revised upward tomorrow: yesterday's similarly dramatic drop at Mattawa (sixteen centimetres, to 152.17 m) was subsequently revised twenty-one centimetres upward once confirmed. The gauge at Pembroke, well downstream of Mattawa, fell only five centimetres — an inconsistency that confirms no real basin-wide flow reduction is behind the Mattawa reading. We will track the revision in tomorrow's brief.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

Today brought a decisive operational shift at the Bryson Generating Station, which sits several kilometres downstream of where Lac Coulonge's southern arm narrows and winds its way down to the dam. After two days of oscillating spill policy, operators sharply increased the flow through the dam's spillway gates — routing water around the turbines — from about 67 cubic metres per second yesterday to about 159 cubic metres per second today. That is more than a doubling of spill in a single day, and it pushes total water passage through the dam to about 446 cubic metres per second, the highest since the Canada Day surge earlier in July.

The effect on the dam pond behind the Bryson dam is immediate and dramatic: the water level in the pond dropped twenty-seven centimetres in a single day from the all-time high set yesterday. At 105.31 metres, the dam pond remains sixty-four centimetres above its normal summer operating ceiling of 104.67 metres, and it has been above that ceiling for sixty consecutive days, but the trend has now reversed. Operators are actively drawing the pond down after the sharp cut two days ago allowed it to rise to a new record high.

The lake gauge at Fort-Coulonge confirms how these two bodies of water respond to each other, even though they are linked only by a long, winding, and narrowing channel. Yesterday's reduced spill at the dam caused the lake to tick upward through the night, and the midnight reading at the start of today matched yesterday's exactly at 106.08 metres — the lake was essentially at a plateau. By late afternoon local time, with the dam's dramatically increased spill pulling more water through the downstream channel, the lake had already eased four centimetres from that plateau to 106.04 metres. The turn is underway.

At 106.04 metres, the lake remains comfortably within normal summer conditions — it sits approximately ninety-six centimetres below the lowest flood-advisory threshold of 107.00 metres, and no flood concern exists whatsoever. The ORRPB's current forecast, unchanged since July 21, calls for stable conditions along the main stem through the coming week. Tomorrow (July 28) brings the first scheduled ORRPB update since July 21, and tomorrow's brief will capture any new forecast language.
