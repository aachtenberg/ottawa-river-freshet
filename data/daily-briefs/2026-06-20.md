## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The third wave appears to have crested. The gauge at Mattawa — where flows from the northern half of the Ottawa's drainage area merge with the main stem — stood at 152.74 metres at midnight, down 4 centimetres from the revised June 19 reading of 152.78 metres. This is the first retreat after the pulse that built steadily through mid-June. The revised peak (152.78 m, June 19) remains 32 centimetres below the spring's primary flood crest in early June. Whether the decline holds over the next few days will be the key indicator of whether the third wave has fully spent itself.

At the head of the basin near the Quebec-Ontario border, outflow from Lake Temiscaming has continued a slow, steady climb: 786 cubic metres per second today, up from 767 two days ago and 778 yesterday. This is now the fourth consecutive day above the 700 mark. Despite elevated outflow, the lake level itself rose very slightly overnight, indicating that tributary inflows into the upper basin are keeping pace with releases — likely the forecast precipitation working through. Reservoir levels across the basin are remarkably stable: all 14 monitored impoundments changed by no more than 2 centimetres overnight, with none falling significantly. The large reservoir that had been absorbing the mid-valley pulse (the Des Joachims impoundment) has returned to equilibrium.

Mid-valley, the town of Pembroke's gauge continued rising by 4 centimetres to 111.63 metres as the Des Joachims dam's pulse finishes routing through. Downstream, the basin's terminal dam at Carillon surged 6.5% in its release rate to over 2,400 cubic metres per second — this is a regulatory decision by Hydro-Québec rather than a direct inflow event, as upstream flows into the lower Ottawa valley are actually easing.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The lake at the foot of Lac Coulonge rose about 5 centimetres today to 106.31 metres as of the live gauge reading at 4 PM. This reverses the flat period of the past few days and is likely reflecting precipitation in the upper watershed working into the system. The lake remains well below any flood threshold, with the nearest alert level still nearly 70 centimetres away.

There is discouraging news about the Bryson dam that controls the lake's outflow. Yesterday's brief noted what appeared to be the first decline in the dam's backed-up headpond after 22 days — a 3-centimetre drop that offered some hope of the system beginning to ease. That hope was short-lived: the headpond rose 4 centimetres today to reach 105.55 metres, a new all-time high for the breach now in its 23rd day. The water behind the dam is now 88 centimetres above the dam's permitted upper operating limit of 104.67 metres. The turbines remain completely offline for the sixth consecutive day, with all 493 cubic metres per second of flow going through the open spillway. Until turbines restart and allow more precise flow management, headpond recovery depends entirely on whether natural inflows diminish — and at present they are not.

The Ottawa River Regulation Planning Board's weekly forecast — last updated June 17 and not due again until June 23 — retains its warning that gradual increases are possible with rapid increases possible if heavy rainfall arrives. Today, the forecast page includes a new sentence directing readers to a "summertime message" on the board's home page, suggesting ORRPB may be preparing to shift toward its lower-frequency summer monitoring posture. For the lakeshore community, conditions remain a watch: no immediate flood risk, but the dam continues to operate under abnormal conditions with no publicly announced timetable for turbine restart.

The headpond reaching a new all-time high of 105.55 m — 88 cm above the permitted ceiling — under continued turbine outage suggests the situation is structural: without turbine operation, any inflow increase directly raises the headpond. This implies ~331 m³/s of unaccounted inflow into Lake Temiscaming from other sub-basins and tributaries. This is consistent with precipitation across the northern Quebec/Ontario basin and explains why Témiscaming outflow has been slowly climbing rather than declining.

The Carillon amont level falling 6 cm suggests meaningful storage draw. This is typical late-season management to hold Lake St. Lawrence (downstream) at desired levels.
