## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The upper section of the Ottawa River's Hydro-Québec dam cascade surged significantly today. Three dams in the upper chain — Première-Chute, the Quinze complex, and the Îles facility — all increased their releases by roughly 20 to 25 percent compared to yesterday's snapshot, and the outlet of Lake Timiscaming (the large lake straddling the Ontario-Québec border near the headwaters) rose to an estimated 617 cubic metres per second — up from 522 yesterday. That's the highest the Timiscaming outflow has been in about a week, and it ends a six-day run during which it had stayed below 600 m³/s. Whether this represents a brief pulse from recent rainfall working through the watershed or the start of a sustained uptick is unclear; the data for today is preliminary and subject to revision, as has happened with prior readings. We are now 61 days past the freshet's primary peak in early May.

The mid-valley reach felt the change overnight. The gauge at Mattawa — roughly halfway down the valley, well above the main dam chain — rose 24 centimetres by midnight to 152.65 metres (a substantial jump, compounded by an upward revision to yesterday's midnight baseline: the database now records June 30 at 152.41 m, 26 cm higher than the value reported in yesterday's brief). Pembroke, further southeast along the main stem, nudged up 4 cm to 111.35 metres — a reversal from the declines it had been posting. The basin's reservoirs shifted from the very settled picture of yesterday (just one falling, eleven steady, one rising) to a broader absorption posture: six reservoirs rising, seven steady, one falling. Reservoirs filling during a post-peak period is a normal and expected refill behaviour — operators are holding back some incoming water rather than passing all of it downstream. Lake Timiscaming itself rose 8 cm despite sending out more water than the upstream Quinze dam was releasing; the difference is attributed to uncontrolled runoff from surrounding tributaries still reaching the lake.

Separately, Carillon — the basin's terminal dam near the Quebec-Ontario border — cut its releases by about 14 percent (from 1,680 to 1,449 cubic metres per second) while the dams upstream were all releasing more. This is a deliberate operational divergence, likely to manage downstream conditions in the Ottawa/Gatineau area. It doesn't affect the upper basin water budget but is worth watching over coming days.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The government telemetry gauge tracking conditions at the Mansfield waterfront — located on Lac Coulonge itself, separate from the dam site several kilometres down the lake's narrowing south channel — read 106.194 metres at 4 PM Eastern time today. That is about 7 centimetres higher than the same hour yesterday (106.128 m), and the midnight official reading confirmed the same upward nudge (+6 cm to 106.19 m versus June 30's midnight value of 106.13 m). The rise is small but real, consistent with the upstream pulse now reaching the property reach. The lake remains in fully normal condition, nearly 81 centimetres below the pre-alert advisory level of 107.00 metres — no flood concern of any kind.

At the dam pond of the Bryson Generating Station — reached by following the lake's south end as it narrows and winds several kilometres downstream to where Hydro-Québec's dam sits — turbines entered their third day of operation. The dam pond level eased 8 centimetres to 105.29 metres, the second consecutive day of decline. The turbines ran at about 288 cubic metres per second — a level established late yesterday evening after a second ramp-up (yesterday's brief captured the earlier 128 m³/s setting; by evening, Hydro-Québec had already scaled up to ~292 m³/s). Despite continued turbine output, the dam pond remains 62 centimetres above the operators' permitted upper ceiling of 104.67 metres, now on Day 34 of that exceedance. The dam's total release increased to 520 m³/s — about 49 m³/s higher than yesterday's snapshot — partly because additional inflow from the upper cascade (which surged 20-25% today) requires more to be released through both turbines and the spillway.

The ORRPB's June 30 forecast remains in place (next update July 7) and calls for "continued gradual decrease or stable" conditions — a direction that is not fully reflected in today's data, which shows modest rises at multiple stations. The broader trend since early May remains clearly downward; today's readings appear to be a temporary perturbation from a rainfall or snowmelt pulse moving through, not a reversal of the season.
