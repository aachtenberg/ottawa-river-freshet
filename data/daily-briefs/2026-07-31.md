## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The long post-freshet retreat at the top of the watershed is now approaching a notable threshold: today's first-published outflow from Lake Timiscaming — the large lake straddling the Ontario-Quebec border where the upper Ottawa River begins its main descent — clocked in at 301 cubic metres per second by one measurement and 306 by a simultaneous cross-check. Both readings sit just above the 300 cubic metres per second level, which would be the lowest single-day figure since before the spring flood season got underway. A word of caution: readings from this station on their first day have been consistently revised downward over the past two weeks — by 12 cubic metres per second the day before yesterday, 25 the week before that. If that pattern holds, tomorrow's confirmed figure could land somewhere in the 280–290 range, firmly below 300. That would mark ninety-one days after the freshet's spring peak near 2,741 cubic metres per second and the nineteenth straight day below 400.

Yesterday's reading, published in last night's brief as 327 cubic metres per second, was revised today to 315 — a 12-unit correction, on the smaller end of what has become a predictable daily revision. Looking across the week: 342 seven days ago, confirmed 315 yesterday, around 300 today provisionally. The trend is a slow, steady decline. Across the basin's fourteen tracked storage reservoirs, nothing moved overnight in any meaningful sense: every measured site sits within one centimetre of yesterday's level, placing the watershed firmly in its quiet, post-flood summer holding pattern.

Mid-valley, the Mattawa gauge reads 152.16 metres in its first published form — a provisional figure that has been revised upward by 20 or more centimetres on every single day of this monitoring period; the confirmed reading due tomorrow will almost certainly sit closer to 152.35–152.55 metres. Pembroke, further downstream and more reliable in its same-day values, is flat at 111.12 metres — unchanged from yesterday's now-confirmed level, which itself was revised up 5 centimetres from the 111.07 reported last night.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

The gauge at Fort-Coulonge reads 106.077 metres as of 4 PM local time — up about 3 centimetres from the same reading 24 hours earlier. The lake is still ticking gently upward in the wake of last weekend's rain event, but at less than a hand's width per day and almost a metre below the lowest flood-advisory level of 107.00 metres. There is no flood concern here, and the slow rate of rise is consistent with the tail end of the rain pulse draining through the system; a reversal is likely within a few days.

At the Bryson Generating Station — which sits well downstream of where the southern end of Lac Coulonge narrows into the river channel, not adjacent to the shoreline properties — operators nudged their spillway open a little further today: the spillway is now passing 142 cubic metres per second, up from 134 yesterday, while the turbines held nearly flat at 287. The pond behind the dam edged up another centimetre to 105.38 metres, now in its 64th consecutive day above the normal summer operating ceiling of 104.67 metres and 71 centimetres above that limit. The incremental spill increase appears to reflect operators matching the tail end of inflow from the Coulonge watershed. The operating band breach continues without remedy, but nothing changed dramatically today.

The most significant development today is downstream at Carillon, the basin-terminal dam more than 300 kilometres to the southeast: after three consecutive days of sharp rises (including a new monitoring-period high of 1,298 cubic metres per second yesterday), Carillon dropped back 76 cubic metres per second to 1,222 today. The two Gatineau River stations immediately above Carillon, which had been driving yesterday's surge, also pulled back — the Rapides-des-Farmers site fell about 13 cubic metres per second. The rain event that swept through July 28–29 has now clearly peaked at the basin terminal and is receding. The Ottawa River Regulation Planning Board's forecast has not been updated since July 28, when it referred to precipitation "today and tomorrow" — both days now in the past. Next update is August 4.

## Notes

- **Carillon reversal mechanics.** Three consecutive days of 1,000+ m³/s (Jul28: ~1,006, Jul29: ~1,117, Jul30: 1,298), all with 0% spill — the dam passed the entire Gatineau rain surge through turbines. Today's 1,222 m³/s reflects Rapides-Farmers pulling back (−13 m³/s), with Bryson's ~429 m³/s forming the Ottawa main-stem base. If Gatineau sites continue retreating toward their pre-rain-event levels (~165–180 m³/s), Carillon should follow toward the 800–900 m³/s range over the next several days — still elevated but not anomalous.

- **Témiscaming sub-300 watch.** The confluence of 301 (ORRPB) and 306 (PSPC) provisional readings, combined with the well-documented overestimate bias (Jul30: −12 m³/s, Jul29: −25 m³/s, Jul28: −60 m³/s cumulative), makes sub-300 confirmation tomorrow quite likely. The declining size of revisions suggests we're entering a regime where provisional values are more accurate — the 280–295 range is a reasonable expectation. If confirmed, update the milestone table with Day 1 sub-300 tomorrow.

- **Bryson tailwater rise (+8 cm) and Carillon backwater.** The Ottawa River between Bryson and Carillon is a partially regulated reach. When Carillon passes extremely high flows (~1,300 m³/s), the resulting elevated downstream water level can back up slightly through the intermediate reach. As Carillon retreats, this backwater effect should ease and the tailwater should return toward the 87.10–87.15 m range, recovering the head differential.

- **ORRPB weekly-notice cadence and the rain event.** The July 28 forecast referred to "today and tomorrow" precipitation in the context of rain arriving that same day. Carillon subsequently rose 29% over two days to a new monitoring-period high. The next ORRPB update is August 4 — by which point the forecast will be 7 days old and this entire rain event and its Carillon spike will have been captured nowhere in the public forecast. This continues to illustrate the operational gap of weekly cadencing during active mid-summer events.
