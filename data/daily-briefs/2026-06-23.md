## In plain language

### Upstream — the upper basin

The upper basin is definitively easing. The surge that crested around June 20th — when outflow from Lake Temiscaming peaked at roughly 784 cubic metres per second — has been draining steadily all week. Today's reading of 748 m³/s is the seventh consecutive day above 700, but the direction has been consistently downward since June 20. Worth noting: the "843 m³/s peak on June 21" reported in earlier briefs turned out to be an initial estimate that was substantially revised once the authoritative federal water-monitoring data was finalized; 784 m³/s on June 20 is the corrected figure. The picture is a bit less dramatic than first appeared, but still a meaningful pulse through the system.

That pulse has now mostly passed through the mid-valley reach. The gauge at Mattawa — about 200 kilometres downstream of Lake Temiscaming — dropped ten centimetres overnight to 152.64 metres, its sharpest single-day decline in several weeks. Pembroke, further down, edged barely lower as well (down 2 cm). Meanwhile, the reservoir at Des Joachims, the mid-river pool managed by Ontario Power Generation, rose 7 cm — operators there are absorbing somewhat more inflow than they are releasing, a normal part of routing flow through a large regulated lake. Across the full basin, 13 of the 14 tracked reservoirs held essentially flat overnight; only Des Joachims is rising, and at 7 cm it is well below the threshold that would indicate operators are actively holding back water at a significant scale.

The generating stations along the upper cascade are clearly stepping down. The Première-Chute facility — the first major dam below Lake Temiscaming — cut its output by more than 12 percent compared to yesterday. The Îles facility further downstream also dropped about 5 percent, and Paugan, on the Gatineau River tributary, fell nearly 10 percent. These reductions reflect operators responding to the declining inflow from the upper basin rather than any independent decision to accumulate storage.

### At the property — Lac Coulonge / Mansfield

Today brought the most encouraging news of the post-flood period for Lac Coulonge proper. The lake dropped roughly 10.6 centimetres between 4 PM yesterday and 4 PM today — the largest single-day decline recorded in these briefs. At 106.22 metres, the lake is fully in normal territory, 78 centimetres below the pre-alert threshold of 107 metres. Recession is clearly underway.

The dam pond at the foot of Lac Coulonge — the backed-up water surface in front of the Bryson Generating Station — is falling faster still. After its all-time high of 105.56 metres on June 21st, the pond dropped 9 cm on June 22nd and another 12 cm today, for a combined 21 cm over two days. At 105.35 metres, it is still 68 centimetres above the operator's permitted upper ceiling of 104.67 metres, and the turbines remain fully offline for the ninth consecutive day. However, the pace of drawdown is accelerating. The operators are releasing about 514 cubic metres per second through the open spillway — essentially unchanged from yesterday — but the flow arriving from upstream is decreasing as the upper cascade steps down. If the current pace continues, the pond could approach the operating band within roughly a week, though actual timing depends heavily on whether any new rainfall enters the upper basin before then. No public statement about turbine restart timing has been made.

The Ottawa River Regulation Planning Board issued its first forecast update in six days this afternoon (3:43 PM local time), replacing the previous text — which said levels "are expected to increase gradually or remain fairly stable" — with a clear statement that they "are expected to decrease gradually or remain fairly stable over the coming week." This is the first time since the freshet peak that the board's official forecast explicitly projects declining conditions. The board has also confirmed it is moving to its summer monitoring cadence: the next update is scheduled for June 30th, seven days from now.
