# Why did 2022 and 2023 produce such different peaks from such similar volumes?

**Compiled 2026-05-27 in response to Larry Norman's question on the FB "Big Picture" thread about the scatter plot in [Carillon peaks + volume narrative](2026-05-27_carillon_peaks_volume_narrative.md), Figure 4. Larry asked: "Why such flipped outcomes between 2022 and 2023? Was it timing of input, changes in initial conditions of reservoirs?"**

## In one line

**Timing, not reservoirs. 2023 had MORE upstream storage capacity available than 2022, yet produced a peak 52% higher from essentially the same total freshet volume. The signature is already visible at Britannia (upstream of Ottawa), which means it is upper-basin meteorology, not a lower-basin Gatineau pulse, and not anything reservoirs could have changed at April 1.**

## The two years on paper

Carillon outflow totals from the ORRPB historical summary:

| Year | Peak (cms) | Apr+May+Jun volume (BCM) | Apr mean | May mean | Jun mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 5,275 | 31.2 | 4,317 | 3,990 | 3,600 |
| 2023 | 8,015 | 30.1 | 4,867 | 5,013 | 1,556 |

Total freshet volume is within 4% (30.1 vs 31.2 BCM). Peak is 52% higher in 2023. That is the year-over-year mystery Larry is asking about.

The monthly means already give a strong hint. 2022 was a flat, sustained freshet (April 4,317 declining to June 3,600, only a 17% drop). 2023 was front-loaded and sharp (April-May around 5,000, then June collapsed to 1,556, a 69% drop). **The same total water, packed into two months instead of three.**

## NOT initial reservoir conditions

The case file has April-1 reservoir state for all 13 principal cascade reservoirs going back to 1990, computed as percent of operating band. Side-by-side 2022 vs 2023:

| Reservoir | 2022 (% of band) | 2023 (% of band) | 2023 minus 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bark Lake | 40.1% | **8.4%** | **-31.8** |
| Cabonga | 69.3% | 69.1% | -0.2 |
| Baskatong | 9.7% | 7.6% | -2.1 |
| Dozois | 22.4% | 20.1% | -2.3 |
| Kiamika | 54.0% | 51.5% | -2.5 |
| Mitchinamecus | 49.5% | 49.1% | -0.4 |
| Quinze | 27.4% | 23.6% | -3.8 |
| Poisson Blanc | 13.3% | 9.5% | -3.8 |
| Timiskaming | 8.6% | 3.0% | -5.7 |
| Kipawa | 15.7% | **5.2%** | **-10.4** |
| Lady Evelyn | 45.2% | 52.8% | +7.6 |
| Rapide-7 | 23.9% | 33.8% | +9.9 |
| Des Joachims | 5.3% | 8.3% | +3.0 |

Most reservoirs are within 4 percentage points of each other on April 1. The two largest divergences are **Bark Lake and Kipawa, both more drawn down in 2023**, meaning *more* buffer capacity was available going into the 2023 freshet, not less. Lady Evelyn and Rapide-7 were slightly higher in 2023 but offer trivial buffering against a basin-scale freshet.

If reservoir state were the driver, 2023 should have produced the lower peak because it had more empty storage available. It produced the higher peak. **Initial conditions are eliminated.**

## YES timing of input. The signature is already upstream of Ottawa.

WSC station 02KF005 at Britannia is the case file's upper-basin proxy. It sits on the Ottawa River roughly 70 km upstream of Ottawa-Gatineau, before the Gatineau confluence. Britannia data isolates the upper Ottawa main stem from the lower-basin tributaries.

Britannia daily flows for the 2022 and 2023 freshet windows:

| | 2022 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Peak | 3,180 cms on Apr 30 | **5,140 cms on May 6** |
| April mean | 2,488 | 2,824 |
| May mean | 2,287 | **3,291** |
| June mean | 2,060 | **1,078** |

The peakedness signature is already there at Britannia. 2023's peak was 62% higher than 2022's, on a similar volume profile. The compression into April-May with collapsing June is visible upstream.

Because the signature exists at Britannia, **it cannot have been caused by a coincident lower-basin tributary pulse** (Gatineau, Lievre, Rideau, or Madawaska, all of which join below Britannia). It is an upper-basin meteorology signal: most likely a temperature pattern that compressed the melt window, possibly amplified by rain-on-snow events, and the absence of recovery precipitation in June.

## What about Steve Deon's Swisha analysis?

Larry noted that the scatter plot at Carillon shows "a radically different relationship than what Steve Deon had for Swisha." This is consistent and not contradictory. Swisha (the headpond immediately above Des Joachims) integrates only the upper Ottawa main stem from Lac Temiskaming down to Des Joachims. Carillon integrates the entire basin including the Gatineau, Lievre, Madawaska, and other lower-basin tributaries.

Two different geographic cuts of the same system can legitimately show different peak-vs-volume relationships:

- At **Swisha**, you are observing the freshet shape of one relatively homogeneous source (upper Ottawa). The peak-vs-volume relationship reflects the meteorology of that single sub-basin and should be tight.
- At **Carillon**, you are observing the sum of several freshets that can be additive or smoothing depending on whether their timing aligns. The peak-vs-volume relationship is looser because more independent inputs are being summed.

Steve's tighter Swisha relationship is therefore expected. The Carillon scatter is not "wrong"; it just integrates more variability. Both stations carry information for different questions:

- **Swisha** is the right station for understanding physical drivers of the upper-basin freshet (timing, melt rate, rain-on-snow at the headwater).
- **Carillon** is the right station for understanding what reaches downstream populations (Hawkesbury, Lac des Deux-Montagnes, the lower Ottawa, and Montreal).

For Larry's specific question, both stations agree on the diagnosis: 2023 was peakier than 2022 at every gauge level we can check. The cause is meteorology, not operations.

## What we cannot test from this data alone

This note rules out reservoir state and identifies timing as the driver. It does not directly identify the *physical* cause of the 2023 timing compression. Candidates worth investigating:

- **Temperature pattern.** Cold late March + warm late April could delay snowpack release and then compress it. Testable from ECCC daily Max/Min temp data we have for nine basin stations.
- **Snow water equivalent at March 31.** Was the 2023 snowpack actually larger, just delivered faster? Or smaller but in a more meltable state? Testable from the ECCC Snow-on-Ground daily column.
- **Rain-on-snow.** A warm rain event in April or early May can compress runoff. Testable from ECCC Total Rain on days with Snow on Ground > 0.
- **Pre-freshet ground saturation.** Wet fall 2022 vs dry fall 2021. Testable from ECCC daily precip back to fall of each year.

All four are answerable from the ECCC archive we already have. They are on the case-file backlog as a follow-on to Steve Laycock's precipitation overlay question. The current note answers Larry's "was it reservoirs?" question definitively (no) and "was it timing?" definitively (yes), without yet attributing the timing to a specific physical mechanism.
